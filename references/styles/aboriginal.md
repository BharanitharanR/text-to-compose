# Aboriginal Australian-inspired (original homage only)

Reach for it: land, ancestry, deep time, red earth, a night sky, a journey on foot, continuity across generations.

## The rule that governs everything

Aboriginal music is tied to specific peoples, Countries and ceremonies; many songs are restricted and belong to their custodians. Never reproduce or imitate a specific song, songline, clan rhythm or ceremonial material. What this file permits is an **original homage**: borrowing instrument sounds and broad textural ideas in a respectful, clearly original piece. Say in the brief: "an original composition drawing on instrument textures associated with Aboriginal Australian music; not a traditional song; consult First Nations practitioners before any public or ceremonial use."

## Textural ideas (broad and widely described)

- A **continuous drone** with rhythmic pulsing and overtone shifts (yidaki / didgeridoo). The drone breathes; it is not a static organ tone.
- **Clapsticks** (bilma): hardwood sticks, steady or interlocking pulse, dry and bright.
- **Call and response**: a leading voice and an answering group.
- Melody with a **descending contour**, falling from a high start to a low rest over the phrase, is a broadly described feature of some traditional song styles; use it as an original gesture, not a copy.
- Few words, repeated; spaciousness and repetition over development.

## Instruments

- **Yidaki / didgeridoo**: drone with rhythmic tonguing, vocalised animal sounds, breath pulses via circular breathing. In the prompt: "didgeridoo drone with rhythmic pulses". In MIDI, a low sustained tone (around D2 to F2) with amplitude pulsing.
- **Clapsticks**: short dry wooden clicks.
- **Bullroarer**: whirring low pitch; sacred in many contexts, so avoid it.
- Ambience: wind over open ground, insects at dusk, distant birds (currawong, magpie), fire.

## Form for an homage

Prelude: ambience, then the drone alone, then clapsticks. Narration over the drone. Verse: a single voice with a falling contour, clapsticks steady, drone pulsing. Conclusion: the drone thins, the sticks stop, ambience remains.

## Fusion partners

Drone under a solo cello or low flute; clapsticks with a frame drum; a single sustained vocal note from another tradition over the drone.

## What outsiders get wrong

- Treating "Aboriginal" as one culture; there are hundreds of nations and languages. Keep the homage generic in origin and say so.
- Using sacred instruments or motifs as texture.
- Adding chords. Drone and voice.
- Inventing fake "Aboriginal" words or vocables. Use the user's own text, or clearly wordless open vowels marked as vocables.
