# West African (Mande, Yoruba, Ewe, Wolof and related traditions)

Reach for it: celebration, lineage and praise, work and community, a gathering, a journey by road, joy that carries history in it, a story told by a griot.

## Rhythmic logic first

West African music is organised around a **timeline** (bell pattern) and interlocking parts, not around a melody with accompaniment.

- The 12/8 standard bell pattern: x . x . x x . x . x . x (strike on 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12 of twelve pulses). Everything else locks to it.
- In 4/4 a common clave-like timeline: x . . x . . x . . . x . x . . .
- Parts enter one at a time, each a short repeating cell (ostinato); the music thickens by addition, not by chord change.
- Call and response between a lead voice or instrument and a chorus is structural.

## Melody and harmony

- Pentatonic and heptatonic scales both occur; Mande kora music often sits in a major-feeling heptatonic with a constant tonic-dominant movement.
- Harmony is cyclic: two or four repeating chords (I - IV, or I - V - IV - I), not a progression that "goes somewhere". The lift comes from density, not modulation.

## Instruments

- **Kora** (21-string harp-lute, Mande): flowing arpeggiated ostinato (kumbengo) with improvised runs (birimintingo). The griot's instrument; praise, genealogy, elegy.
- **Balafon**: gourd-resonated xylophone, buzzing tone, interlocking patterns.
- **Ngoni**: plucked lute, dry and rhythmic.
- **Djembe**: goblet drum, three tones (bass, tone, slap); lead and accompaniment parts.
- **Dunun** (dundun, sangban, kenkeni): bass drums with bells; carry the timeline.
- **Talking drum** (tama, dundun of the Yoruba): pitch bends imitating speech; use it to "speak" a phrase of the text.
- **Shekere** (beaded gourd), **gankogui** (double bell).
- Ambience: a courtyard, children, a market, rain on tin, a river crossing, a bus horn.

## Singing

Lead singer (often the griot) with a declamatory, slightly nasal, powerful tone; chorus answers in unison or parallel thirds. Praise songs name the person and their lineage; a text addressed to someone becomes a praise song naturally. Languages: Bambara, Wolof, Yoruba, Ewe, French, English.

## Form for a song

Prelude: ambience, kora ostinato alone, narrator speaks over it. Verse: lead sings the text, chorus answers with a short repeated phrase drawn from the turn line, percussion enters part by part. Conclusion: parts drop out in reverse order until only the kora remains, then one final bell stroke.

## Fusion partners

Kora with a Celtic harp; balafon with a Carnatic ghatam; the 12/8 bell under a Hindustani tabla cycle; a talking drum "speaking" a Tamil line.

## What outsiders get wrong

- Treating drums as the whole thing; the kora and the voice carry the meaning.
- Harmonic progressions that resolve; keep the cycle.
- Generic "African drums" descriptions. Name the instruments and the timeline.
- Forgetting the response: a West African song without a chorus answer is half a song.
